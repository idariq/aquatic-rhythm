The Fertiliser Dose
You Missed

A skipped dose behaves nothing like a skipped water change.

A missed water change means waste builds up. A missed fertiliser dose is the opposite kind of gap — plants keep drawing down nutrients that were never topped back up.

This guide explains what actually happens when a dose is missed, why the risk is often algae rather than just slower growth, and how to get back on schedule without overdosing to compensate.

4 modules· ~6 min· Practical
01 / 04A different kind of miss

Nutrients don't build up.
They run out.

A missed water change and a missed fertiliser dose feel similar, but they work in opposite directions. Skip a water change, and waste and nitrate accumulate in the water. Skip a dose, and something is instead removed from the water faster than it's replaced.

Plants don't pause consuming nutrients just because dosing paused. They keep pulling whatever's already dissolved in the water until it's gone. A missed dose doesn't add a problem to the tank. It quietly empties a resource the plants were already relying on.

That difference matters for how you respond. The instinct that works for a missed water change — do a normal-sized one and move on — isn't quite right here. There's nothing to flush out. There's only a gap to refill.

Don't double the next dose

Doubling a dose to "catch up" pushes several nutrients up at once, and it's the ratio between them, not any single number, that plants and algae actually respond to. Resuming the normal dose does more good than compensating for the gap.

02 / 04What actually happens

A short gap is invisible.
A long one shows up as algae.

A day or two without dosing is rarely noticeable. Plants draw on whatever's still dissolved in the water column, and that reserve absorbs a short gap without any visible change.

A longer gap is a different story. Beyond a week or so, specific deficiency symptoms can appear — yellowing between leaf veins, pinholes, or new growth that comes in smaller and paler than usual. Which symptom shows up first depends on which nutrient runs out first, and that varies by dosing routine and plant mix.

The less obvious risk is algae, not just slower growth. Algae often exploits an unstable nutrient balance more readily than plants do. This is especially true in a CO2-injected tank, where light, CO2, and nutrients all need to stay in proportion. A gap that throws that balance off can trigger algae even while overall nutrient levels look fine on paper.

"A missed dose doesn't punish a tank the way a missed water change might. It just quietly asks the plants to wait a little longer for their next meal — and waiting too long is what actually causes the trouble."

03 / 04When it matters most

High-tech tanks
feel it fastest.

CO2-injected, high-light tanks feel a missed dose soonest. Fast growth under strong light means plants pull nutrients quickly, and dosing methods built around running lean, like EI dosing, have little buffer by design. A missed dose there shows up in days, not weeks.

Heavily planted tanks in general draw down nutrients faster than lightly planted ones, simply because there's more plant mass consuming them. A tank with a few slow-growing plants under moderate light has far more slack.

Low-tech, lightly planted tanks have the most room to absorb a gap. Growth is slower there by design, so nutrient demand is lower too, and a missed week or two often passes without any visible effect at all.

Watch the plants, not just a test kit

Nutrient test kits exist, but they're less reliable and less commonly used than ammonia or nitrate kits. New growth is often the more practical signal — check its colour and size against what the plant was producing a couple of weeks ago.

04 / 04Getting back on schedule

Resume the normal dose.
Don't chase the gap.

In most cases, the aligned response is simply to resume dosing at the normal amount and normal schedule. There's no deficit to erase in one go, and pushing a bigger dose to compensate risks tipping the nutrient ratio rather than restoring it.

If new growth already shows a clear deficiency symptom, one somewhat larger dose can help correct it, but it still shouldn't become the new normal. Watch the next round of growth over the following week or two to see whether the symptom resolves before adjusting anything further.

Going forward, the fix is the same one that helps with any missed routine: remove the dependence on memory. A dosing pump, or a reminder placed somewhere you'll actually see it, does more for long-term consistency than any single corrective dose ever could.

"The plants don't grade you on the dose you missed either. They just respond to whatever balance they're actually given, one week at a time."
